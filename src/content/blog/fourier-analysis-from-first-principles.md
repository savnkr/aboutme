## Notation and conventions

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x$ | position or time; $\boldsymbol{x} = (x, y)$ in 2-D |
| $\xi$ | frequency, in cycles per unit; $\boldsymbol{\xi} = (\xi, \eta)$ in 2-D |
| $e_k(x) = e^{2\pi i k x}$ | the Fourier basis on $[0, 1)$ |
| $\langle f, g\rangle = \int f\, \overline{g}$ | inner product |
| $c_k = \langle f, e_k \rangle$ | Fourier coefficient |
| $S_N f = \sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N} c_k e_k$ | partial sum |
| $\hat f(\xi) = \int f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i x \xi}\, dx$ | Fourier transform |
| $(f * g)(x) = \int f(y)\, g(x - y)\, dy$ | convolution |
| $\operatorname{rect}(x)$ | $1$ for $\lvert x \rvert < \tfrac12$, else $0$ |
| $\operatorname{sinc}(\xi)$ | $\sin(\pi\xi)/(\pi\xi)$, with $\operatorname{sinc}(0) = 1$ |

Vectors are bold italic, matrices bold upright. Frequencies are in **cycles per unit**, not radians, so the transform and its inverse carry no stray factors of $2\pi$; Section 6 has a table for translating to the $\omega$ convention used in many textbooks.

## 1. Start from what you trust: coordinates

Take a vector $\boldsymbol{v}$ in the plane and two unit vectors $\boldsymbol{u}_1, \boldsymbol{u}_2$ at right angles. Three facts about them will carry the whole post.

:::proposition[Coordinates in an orthonormal basis]
If $\langle \boldsymbol{u}_i, \boldsymbol{u}_j\rangle = 1$ when $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise, then for every $\boldsymbol{v}$:
1. its coordinates are inner products, $a_i = \langle \boldsymbol{v}, \boldsymbol{u}_i\rangle$;
2. it is rebuilt from them, $\boldsymbol{v} = a_1 \boldsymbol{u}_1 + a_2 \boldsymbol{u}_2$;
3. its squared length is the sum of squared coordinates, $\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2$.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. Write $\boldsymbol{v}$ in the basis.** Two orthonormal vectors in the plane span it, so $\boldsymbol{v} = a_1\boldsymbol{u}_1 + a_2\boldsymbol{u}_2$ for some numbers $a_1, a_2$. This is fact 2, once we know what the $a_i$ are.

**Step 2. Take the inner product with $\boldsymbol{u}_1$.** By linearity, $\langle \boldsymbol{v}, \boldsymbol{u}_1\rangle = a_1\langle \boldsymbol{u}_1, \boldsymbol{u}_1\rangle + a_2\langle \boldsymbol{u}_2, \boldsymbol{u}_1\rangle = a_1 \cdot 1 + a_2 \cdot 0 = a_1$. The same with $\boldsymbol{u}_2$ gives $a_2$. This is fact 1.

**Step 3. Expand the length.** $\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^2 = \langle a_1\boldsymbol{u}_1 + a_2\boldsymbol{u}_2,\, a_1\boldsymbol{u}_1 + a_2\boldsymbol{u}_2\rangle = a_1^2\langle\boldsymbol{u}_1,\boldsymbol{u}_1\rangle + 2a_1a_2\langle\boldsymbol{u}_1,\boldsymbol{u}_2\rangle + a_2^2\langle\boldsymbol{u}_2,\boldsymbol{u}_2\rangle = a_1^2 + a_2^2$. This is fact 3: Pythagoras.
:::

:::example[By hand]
Let $\boldsymbol{v} = (3, 1)$, $\boldsymbol{u}_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and $\boldsymbol{u}_2 = (-1, 1)/\sqrt2$. Then $a_1 = (3 + 1)/\sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2$ and $a_2 = (-3 + 1)/\sqrt2 = -\sqrt2$. Rebuild: $2\sqrt2\,\boldsymbol{u}_1 - \sqrt2\,\boldsymbol{u}_2 = (2, 2) - (-1, 1) = (3, 1)$. Lengths: $a_1^2 + a_2^2 = 8 + 2 = 10 = 3^2 + 1^2$.
:::

A coordinate is a shadow: drop a perpendicular from $\boldsymbol{v}$ onto an axis and measure where it lands. Nothing in the three facts needed the plane or two dimensions, only an inner product and an orthonormal set. Fourier analysis is these three facts again, with functions in place of arrows.

## 2. Functions are vectors

Functions on $[0, 1)$ add and scale like vectors: $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$ and $(af)(x) = af(x)$. What they need to become geometry is an inner product.

:::definition[Inner product and norm of functions]
For square-integrable complex functions on $[0, 1)$,
$$
\langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^1 f(x)\, \overline{g(x)}\, dx, \qquad \lVert f\rVert = \sqrt{\langle f, f\rangle} = \Big(\int_0^1 \lvert f(x)\rvert^2\, dx\Big)^{1/2}.
$$
The set of functions with $\lVert f \rVert < \infty$ is called $L^2[0, 1)$.
:::

The bar on $g$ is there so that $\langle f, f\rangle = \int \lvert f\rvert^2$ is real and non-negative, as a squared length must be. Think of $\int_0^1 f\,\overline{g}$ as the dot product $\sum_i f_i \overline{g_i}$ with infinitely many, densely packed coordinates $f_i = f(x_i)$.

Our basis will be the complex exponentials, and we need one fact about them first.

:::definition[The Fourier basis]
For each integer $k$, $e_k(x) = e^{2\pi i k x} = \cos(2\pi k x) + i \sin(2\pi k x)$.
:::

:::lemma[Euler's formula]
For real $\theta$, $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$.
:::

:::proof
Here $e^{z} = \sum_{n \ge 0} z^n / n!$, which converges for every complex $z$ and can be differentiated term by term, so $\frac{d}{d\theta} e^{i\theta} = i e^{i\theta}$ and $\frac{d}{d\theta} e^{-i\theta} = -i e^{-i\theta}$.

**Step 1. A function that should be constant.** Let $g(\theta) = e^{-i\theta}(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$.

**Step 2. Differentiate with the product rule.** $g'(\theta) = -i e^{-i\theta}(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) + e^{-i\theta}(-\sin\theta + i\cos\theta) = e^{-i\theta}\big(-i\cos\theta + \sin\theta - \sin\theta + i\cos\theta\big) = 0$.

**Step 3. So $g$ is constant**, equal to $g(0) = 1 \cdot (1 + 0) = 1$. Multiply $g(\theta) = 1$ by $e^{i\theta}$ (using $e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta} = e^0 = 1$, which follows from the series): $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta = e^{i\theta}$.
:::

:::proposition[The eₖ are orthonormal]
$\langle e_k, e_l\rangle = 1$ if $k = l$, and $0$ if $k \ne l$.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. Multiply.** $e_k(x)\,\overline{e_l(x)} = e^{2\pi i k x} e^{-2\pi i l x} = e^{2\pi i m x}$ with $m = k - l$, an integer. (The conjugate of $e^{i\alpha}$ is $\cos\alpha - i\sin\alpha = e^{-i\alpha}$.)

**Step 2. If $m = 0$**, the integrand is $1$ and $\int_0^1 1\, dx = 1$.

**Step 3. If $m \ne 0$**, an antiderivative is $e^{2\pi i m x}/(2\pi i m)$, so $\int_0^1 e^{2\pi i m x} dx = \frac{e^{2\pi i m} - 1}{2\pi i m} = 0$, because $e^{2\pi i m} = \cos(2\pi m) + i\sin(2\pi m) = 1$ for every integer $m$.
:::

:::question
Why complex exponentials and not plain sines and cosines?
:::

:::answer
Both work. The real set $\{1, \sqrt2\cos 2\pi k x, \sqrt2\sin 2\pi kx\}_{k \ge 1}$ is also orthonormal and gives the same expansions. A wave of one frequency has two numbers, an amplitude and a phase, and a complex coefficient stores exactly those two. The complex form is tidier for three reasons that appear below: products of exponentials add frequencies ($e_k e_l = e_{k+l}$); a shift multiplies $e_k$ by a constant (Section 5), where it would mix a sine with a cosine; and one formula covers every coefficient.
:::

So what does a Fourier coefficient measure? By fact 1, it is the inner product $\langle f, e_k\rangle = \int_0^1 f(x)\cos(2\pi kx)\,dx - i\int_0^1 f(x)\sin(2\pi kx)\,dx$: multiply the signal by a probe wave and add up the area. Try it.

## 3. Fourier series

Apply the three facts of Section 1 with $\boldsymbol{u}_i$ replaced by $e_k$.

:::definition[Fourier coefficients and partial sums]
For $f \in L^2[0, 1)$ (extended periodically to the whole line),
$$
c_k = \langle f, e_k\rangle = \int_0^1 f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i k x}\, dx, \qquad S_N f(x) = \sum_{k=-N}^{N} c_k\, e^{2\pi i k x}.
$$
The **Fourier series** of $f$ is the limit of $S_N f$ as $N \to \infty$.
:::

With infinitely many basis functions there is a new question: does the sum get back to $f$? Fact 2 needs care, and Section 4 answers it. Before that, two things the partial sum gets right for every $N$.

:::proposition[The partial sum is the best approximation]
Among all trigonometric polynomials $p = \sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N} b_k e_k$, the partial sum $S_N f$ is the closest to $f$:
$$
\lVert f - p\rVert^2 = \lVert f - S_N f\rVert^2 + \sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N} \lvert c_k - b_k\rvert^2 \;\ge\; \lVert f - S_N f\rVert^2 .
$$
Taking $p = 0$ gives **Bessel's inequality**, $\sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N} \lvert c_k\rvert^2 \le \lVert f\rVert^2$.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. The error is orthogonal to every $e_k$ with $\lvert k\rvert \le N$.** $\langle f - S_N f, e_k\rangle = \langle f, e_k\rangle - \sum_{\lvert l\rvert \le N} c_l\langle e_l, e_k\rangle = c_k - c_k = 0$, by orthonormality.

**Step 2. So it is orthogonal to $S_N f - p$**, which is the combination $\sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N}(c_k - b_k)e_k$ of those $e_k$.

**Step 3. Pythagoras.** Write $f - p = (f - S_N f) + (S_N f - p)$. Expanding $\lVert f - p\rVert^2$, the two cross terms are inner products of orthogonal functions and vanish, leaving $\lVert f - S_N f\rVert^2 + \lVert S_N f - p\rVert^2$.

**Step 4. The last term**, by fact 3 for the orthonormal $e_k$, is $\sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N}\lvert c_k - b_k\rvert^2$.

**Step 5. Bessel.** With $p = 0$ (all $b_k = 0$): $\lVert f\rVert^2 = \lVert f - S_N f\rVert^2 + \sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N}\lvert c_k\rvert^2 \ge \sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N}\lvert c_k\rvert^2$.
:::

### Negative frequencies are a direction of rotation

Ask a room of engineers what a negative frequency is and someone will say *a mathematical artefact*. It is a direction of rotation. As $x$ increases, $e^{2\pi i k x}$ walks around the unit circle $\lvert k\rvert$ times per unit: anticlockwise if $k > 0$, clockwise if $k < 0$. A real oscillation moves back and forth on a line, and a single rotating point cannot do that. Two can, if they turn in opposite directions at the same speed.

:::proposition[A real signal pairs every frequency with its negative]
If $f$ is real-valued, then $c_{-k} = \overline{c_k}$ for every $k$, the mean $c_0$ is real, and each pair of terms is one real wave:
$$
c_k\, e^{2\pi i k x} + c_{-k}\, e^{-2\pi i k x} = 2\lvert c_k\rvert \cos\!\big(2\pi k x + \arg c_k\big).
$$
Equivalently, $f(x) = c_0 + \sum_{k \ge 1}\big(a_k\cos 2\pi kx + b_k \sin 2\pi kx\big)$ with $a_k = 2\operatorname{Re} c_k = 2\int_0^1 f(x)\cos 2\pi kx\, dx$ and $b_k = -2\operatorname{Im} c_k = 2\int_0^1 f(x)\sin 2\pi kx\, dx$.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. Conjugate the definition.** The conjugate of an integral is the integral of the conjugate: $\overline{c_k} = \int_0^1 \overline{f(x)}\;\overline{e^{-2\pi i k x}}\, dx$.

**Step 2. Use that $f$ is real.** $\overline{f(x)} = f(x)$ and $\overline{e^{-i\alpha}} = e^{i\alpha}$, so $\overline{c_k} = \int_0^1 f(x)\, e^{2\pi i k x}\, dx$.

**Step 3. Recognise $c_{-k}$.** By definition $c_{-k} = \int_0^1 f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i(-k)x}\, dx$, the same integral. So $c_{-k} = \overline{c_k}$, and with $k = 0$, $c_0 = \overline{c_0}$ is real.

**Step 4. Add the pair.** With $\alpha = 2\pi kx$, the second term is $\overline{c_k}\,\overline{e^{i\alpha}} = \overline{c_k e^{i\alpha}}$, and $z + \overline{z} = 2\operatorname{Re} z$. So the pair equals $2\operatorname{Re}(c_k e^{i\alpha})$.

**Step 5. Polar form.** Write $c_k = \lvert c_k\rvert e^{i\varphi_k}$. Then $2\operatorname{Re}(c_k e^{i\alpha}) = 2\lvert c_k\rvert\cos(\alpha + \varphi_k)$.

**Step 6. Cartesian form.** Write $c_k = \alpha_k + i\beta_k$. Then $2\operatorname{Re}\big((\alpha_k + i\beta_k)(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)\big) = 2\alpha_k\cos\alpha - 2\beta_k\sin\alpha$, so $a_k = 2\alpha_k$ and $b_k = -2\beta_k$. From $c_k = \int f\cos 2\pi kx - i\int f\sin 2\pi kx$, $\alpha_k = \int f\cos 2\pi k x$ and $\beta_k = -\int f \sin 2\pi kx$.
:::

So a real wave of amplitude $A$ is two phasors of length $A/2$, one at $+k$ and one at $-k$. That is why the probe above found $\lvert c_3\rvert = 0.45$ for a wave of amplitude $0.9$, and why the FFT of a real cosine shows two peaks, not one (Part 2 works that out by hand).

### The square wave, by hand

:::example[Fourier coefficients of a square wave]
Let $f = 1$ on $[0, \tfrac12)$ and $f = -1$ on $[\tfrac12, 1)$.

**Step 1. The mean.** $c_0 = \int_0^{1/2} 1\, dx - \int_{1/2}^1 1\, dx = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$.

**Step 2. Split the integral** for $k \ne 0$: $c_k = \int_0^{1/2} e^{-2\pi i k x}dx - \int_{1/2}^1 e^{-2\pi i k x}dx$.

**Step 3. First piece.** With the antiderivative $e^{-2\pi i k x}/(-2\pi i k)$: $\frac{e^{-\pi i k} - 1}{-2\pi i k} = \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{2\pi i k}$, using $e^{-\pi i k} = (-1)^k$.

**Step 4. Second piece.** $\frac{e^{-2\pi i k} - e^{-\pi i k}}{-2\pi i k} = \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{-2\pi i k}$.

**Step 5. Subtract.** $c_k = \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{2\pi i k} + \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{2\pi i k} = \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{\pi i k}$: zero for even $k$, and $\frac{2}{\pi i k} = -\frac{2i}{\pi k}$ for odd $k$.

**Step 6. Real form.** $a_k = 2\operatorname{Re} c_k = 0$ and $b_k = -2\operatorname{Im} c_k = \frac{4}{\pi k}$ for odd $k$, so
$$
f(x) = \frac{4}{\pi}\Big(\sin 2\pi x + \frac{\sin 6\pi x}{3} + \frac{\sin 10\pi x}{5} + \cdots\Big).
$$
Numerically, $c_1 = -0.6366i$, $c_3 = -0.2122i$, $c_5 = -0.1273i$: an FFT of 65,536 samples agrees to four decimals.
:::

## 4. How good is a partial sum?

There are two questions, and they have different answers. Does the *energy* of the error go to zero? Yes, for every square-integrable function. Does the error go to zero *at every point*? Not always, and the square wave is the classic failure.

### In energy: always

:::theorem[Convergence in L² and Parseval's identity]
For every $f \in L^2[0, 1)$, $\lVert f - S_N f\rVert \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$, and
$$
\lVert f\rVert^2 = \int_0^1 \lvert f(x)\rvert^2\, dx = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \lvert c_k\rvert^2 .
$$
:::

The proof needs one new tool. The partial sums themselves can misbehave pointwise, but their running averages are well behaved, because they are averages of $f$ against a positive kernel.

:::proof
**Step 1. $S_N f$ is a convolution.** Insert the definition of $c_k$: $S_N f(x) = \sum_{\lvert k\rvert\le N}\big(\int_0^1 f(y)e^{-2\pi i k y}dy\big)e^{2\pi i k x} = \int_0^1 f(y)\, D_N(x - y)\, dy$, with the **Dirichlet kernel** $D_N(u) = \sum_{\lvert k\rvert \le N} e^{2\pi i k u}$.

**Step 2. Closed form of $D_N$.** With $q = e^{2\pi i u} \ne 1$, the geometric sum is $\sum_{k=-N}^{N} q^k = q^{-N}\frac{q^{2N+1} - 1}{q - 1}$. Multiply top and bottom by $q^{-1/2}$: $\frac{q^{N + 1/2} - q^{-N-1/2}}{q^{1/2} - q^{-1/2}} = \frac{\sin((2N+1)\pi u)}{\sin(\pi u)}$, since $e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta$.

**Step 3. Average the partial sums.** The **Fejér mean** is $\sigma_N f = \frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} S_n f = f * F_N$ with $F_N = \frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} D_n$.

**Step 4. Closed form of $F_N$.** By Step 2, $F_N(u) = \frac{1}{N\sin(\pi u)}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sin((2n+1)\pi u)$. The identity $2\sin a \sin((2n+1)a) = \cos(2na) - \cos((2n+2)a)$ makes the sum telescope: $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sin((2n+1)a) = \frac{1 - \cos(2Na)}{2\sin a} = \frac{\sin^2(Na)}{\sin a}$. Hence $F_N(u) = \frac{\sin^2(N\pi u)}{N\sin^2(\pi u)}$.

**Step 5. Three properties of $F_N$.** (a) $F_N \ge 0$, by Step 4. (b) $\int_0^1 F_N = 1$, because each $D_n$ integrates to $1$ (only its $k = 0$ term survives, by orthogonality). (c) For any $0 < \delta < \tfrac12$, on $\delta \le \lvert u\rvert \le \tfrac12$ we have $F_N(u) \le \frac{1}{N\sin^2(\pi\delta)} \to 0$.

**Step 6. Fejér means of continuous $f$ converge uniformly.** By (b), $\sigma_N f(x) - f(x) = \int F_N(u)\big(f(x - u) - f(x)\big)du$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, uniform continuity gives $\delta$ with $\lvert f(x - u) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for $\lvert u\rvert < \delta$. Split the integral: the near part is at most $\varepsilon\int F_N = \varepsilon$ by (a) and (b); the far part is at most $2\max\lvert f\rvert\cdot\frac{1}{N\sin^2(\pi\delta)}$ by (c), which is below $\varepsilon$ for large $N$.

**Step 7. Partial sums of continuous $f$ converge in energy.** $\sigma_N f$ is a trigonometric polynomial of degree below $N$, so by the best-approximation property, $\lVert f - S_N f\rVert \le \lVert f - \sigma_N f\rVert \le \max_x\lvert f(x) - \sigma_N f(x)\rvert \to 0$.

**Step 8. All of $L^2$.** Continuous periodic functions are dense in $L^2[0,1)$ (a standard fact of measure theory). Given $f$ and $\varepsilon$, pick a continuous $g$ with $\lVert f - g\rVert < \varepsilon$. $S_N g$ is a polynomial of degree $N$, so by best approximation $\lVert f - S_N f\rVert \le \lVert f - S_N g\rVert \le \lVert f - g\rVert + \lVert g - S_N g\rVert < 2\varepsilon$ for large $N$.

**Step 9. Parseval.** Step 5 of the best-approximation proof says $\lVert f\rVert^2 = \lVert f - S_N f\rVert^2 + \sum_{\lvert k\rvert\le N}\lvert c_k\rvert^2$. Let $N \to \infty$; the first term goes to $0$.
:::

Two consequences we have already used. The orthonormal set $\{e_k\}$ is **complete**: nothing in $L^2$ is orthogonal to all of it, so fact 2 holds in the energy sense. And the squared error of a partial sum is exactly the energy of the coefficients left out, $\lVert f - S_N f\rVert^2 = \sum_{\lvert k\rvert > N}\lvert c_k\rvert^2$. That is how the epicycle figure at the top prints its error without comparing curves: it adds up the circles it is not drawing.

### Pointwise: the Gibbs phenomenon

Energy convergence allows a thin spike of error to survive, as long as it gets thinner. Beside a jump, it does.

:::proposition[Gibbs overshoot]
For the square wave of Section 3, let $S$ be the partial sum with the odd harmonics $k = 1, 3, \ldots, 2M - 1$. Its first maximum to the right of the jump at $x = 0$ is at $x_M = \frac{1}{4M}$, and as $M \to \infty$,
$$
S(x_M) \to \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\frac{\sin t}{t}\, dt = \frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{Si}(\pi) \approx 1.17898 .
$$
The function jumps from $-1$ to $1$, so the overshoot above $1$ is about $8.95\%$ of the jump, for every $M$ large enough, however many terms are used.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. The partial sum.** From the example in Section 3, $S(x) = \frac4\pi\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\sin(2\pi(2m-1)x)}{2m-1}$.

**Step 2. Its derivative.** $S'(x) = \frac4\pi\sum_{m=1}^M 2\pi\cos(2\pi(2m-1)x) = 8\sum_{m=1}^M\cos((2m-1)\theta)$ with $\theta = 2\pi x$.

**Step 3. Sum the cosines.** The identity $2\sin\theta\cos((2m-1)\theta) = \sin(2m\theta) - \sin((2m-2)\theta)$ (from $2\sin A\cos B = \sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)$) telescopes: $\sum_{m=1}^M\cos((2m-1)\theta) = \frac{\sin(2M\theta)}{2\sin\theta}$. Hence $S'(x) = \frac{4\sin(4\pi M x)}{\sin(2\pi x)}$.

**Step 4. The first maximum.** For small $x > 0$ the denominator is positive and $S' > 0$ until the numerator first vanishes, at $4\pi M x = \pi$. So $S$ rises to a maximum at $x_M = 1/(4M)$.

**Step 5. Evaluate it.** At $x_M$, $2\pi(2m - 1)x_M = t_m$ with $t_m = \frac{(2m-1)\pi}{2M}$, and $\frac{1}{2m-1} = \frac{\pi}{2M t_m}$. So $S(x_M) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{m=1}^M \sin(t_m)\frac{\pi}{2Mt_m} = \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{m=1}^M\frac{\sin t_m}{t_m}\cdot\frac{\pi}{M}$.

**Step 6. Recognise a Riemann sum.** The points $t_m$ are the midpoints of $M$ equal intervals of width $\pi/M$ covering $[0, \pi]$, so the sum tends to $\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin t}{t}dt = \operatorname{Si}(\pi) \approx 1.85194$. Multiplying by $2/\pi$ gives $1.17898$.
:::

Measured values agree: with $M = 5, 10, 50, 200$ odd terms the maximum is $1.18233$, $1.17981$, $1.17901$, $1.17898$, each at $x = 1/(4M)$. The peak moves toward the jump and gets narrower, so its energy vanishes, but its height does not. Where does it come from? Step 1 of the Parseval proof says $S_N f = f * D_N$, and the Dirichlet kernel has negative side lobes. Averaging a jump against a kernel with negative lobes produces ripples. The Fejér kernel has no negative lobes, which is why Fejér means of a square wave never overshoot.

:::note[Gibbs in practice]
The same ripples appear whenever a spectrum is cut off sharply: around edges in an image low-passed with a brick-wall filter (Section 9), in MRI scans reconstructed from a truncated set of frequencies ("truncation artefacts"), and in spectral solvers of PDEs with shocks. The cure is always the same: taper the cut instead of chopping it.
:::

### Smoothness is decay

The second observation from the series figure, that the triangle's coefficients fall faster, is a theorem worth knowing by heart.

:::proposition[Smooth functions have rapidly decaying coefficients]
Let $f$ be periodic with $f, f', \ldots, f^{(p-1)}$ continuous and $f^{(p)}$ piecewise continuous. Then for $k \ne 0$
$$
c_k(f) = \frac{c_k\big(f^{(p)}\big)}{(2\pi i k)^p}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \lvert c_k(f)\rvert \le \frac{\int_0^1\lvert f^{(p)}\rvert}{(2\pi\lvert k\rvert)^p}.
$$
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. One integration by parts.** $c_k(f') = \int_0^1 f'(x)e^{-2\pi i k x}dx = \big[f(x)e^{-2\pi i k x}\big]_0^1 + 2\pi i k\int_0^1 f(x)e^{-2\pi i k x}dx$.

**Step 2. The boundary term vanishes**, because $f$ and $e^{-2\pi i k x}$ take the same values at $0$ and $1$. So $c_k(f') = 2\pi i k\, c_k(f)$.

**Step 3. Repeat** $p$ times (allowed because $f, \ldots, f^{(p-1)}$ are continuous and periodic): $c_k(f^{(p)}) = (2\pi i k)^p c_k(f)$.

**Step 4. Bound.** $\lvert c_k(g)\rvert \le \int_0^1\lvert g(x)\rvert\,\lvert e^{-2\pi i k x}\rvert\,dx = \int_0^1\lvert g\rvert$, since $\lvert e^{i\alpha}\rvert = 1$.
:::

A jump gives $1/k$ decay (the square wave: $\lvert c_k\rvert = 2/(\pi k)$). A kink gives $1/k^2$: the triangle wave $1 - 4\lvert x - \tfrac12\rvert$ has derivative $4$ times the square wave, so its coefficients are $4\cdot\frac{2}{\pi i k}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i k} = -\frac{4}{\pi^2 k^2}$ for odd $k$, which is what the log-scale stems in the figure show. A function with $p$ continuous derivatives decays faster than $1/k^p$, and an infinitely smooth one faster than any power. Read backwards, this is a detector: **slowly decaying coefficients mean a singularity somewhere**, but the coefficients do not say where. Wavelets, in Part 3, will say where.

## 5. Why sinusoids? The real reason

Why this basis? There are many orthonormal bases of $L^2[0,1)$: Legendre polynomials, Haar functions, wavelets. The usual answer, "because signals are made of waves", is not the reason. The reason is that **sinusoids are the only functions that every shift-invariant operation leaves alone**.

Write $T_s$ for the shift, $(T_s f)(x) = f(x - s)$. An operator $L$ on periodic functions is **shift-invariant** if it commutes with every shift: $L T_s = T_s L$. Blurring is shift-invariant (it treats every place alike), so is differentiation, so is any convolution, and so is the Laplacian.

:::proposition[Every shift-invariant linear operator has the eₖ as eigenfunctions]
If $L$ is linear and shift-invariant, then $L e_k = \lambda_k e_k$ for every $k$, with $\lambda_k = (L e_k)(0)$.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. $e_k$ is an eigenfunction of each shift.** $(T_s e_k)(x) = e^{2\pi i k(x - s)} = e^{-2\pi i k s}\,e_k(x)$.

**Step 2. Push the shift through $L$.** Let $g = L e_k$. Then $T_s g = T_s L e_k = L T_s e_k = L\big(e^{-2\pi i k s}e_k\big) = e^{-2\pi i k s}\,g$, using shift-invariance and then linearity.

**Step 3. Read it pointwise.** $g(x - s) = e^{-2\pi i k s}g(x)$ for all $x$ and $s$. Put $x = s$: $g(0) = e^{-2\pi i k s}g(s)$, so $g(s) = g(0)\,e^{2\pi i k s} = g(0)\,e_k(s)$.
:::

So in the Fourier basis, every shift-invariant operator is **diagonal**: it multiplies the $k$-th coordinate by $\lambda_k$ and does nothing else. For a convolution $L f = h * f$ (with $(h * f)(x) = \int_0^1 h(y)f(x - y)\,dy$), $\lambda_k = c_k(h)$, so

$$
c_k(h * f) = c_k(h)\, c_k(f).
$$

For the derivative, $\lambda_k = 2\pi i k$; for the second derivative, $-(2\pi k)^2$, the eigenvalues of the Laplacian (more in [the post on the Laplacian](/aboutme/blog/one-operator-i-keep-coming-back-to/)).

:::proof[the convolution rule cₖ(h ∗ f) = cₖ(h) cₖ(f), directly]
**Step 1. Write it out.** $c_k(h * f) = \int_0^1\Big(\int_0^1 h(y)f(x - y)\,dy\Big)e^{-2\pi i k x}\,dx$.

**Step 2. Split the exponential.** $e^{-2\pi i k x} = e^{-2\pi i k y}\,e^{-2\pi i k(x - y)}$, and swap the order of integration (Fubini; $h, f$ integrable): $\int_0^1 h(y)e^{-2\pi i k y}\Big(\int_0^1 f(x - y)e^{-2\pi i k(x-y)}dx\Big)dy$.

**Step 3. The inner integral is $c_k(f)$**: substitute $u = x - y$; by periodicity, the integral over any interval of length $1$ is the same.

**Step 4. What is left** is $c_k(f)\int_0^1 h(y)e^{-2\pi i k y}dy = c_k(h)\,c_k(f)$.
:::

The converse is what makes the exponentials special, and not just convenient.

:::proposition[The eₖ are the only common eigenfunctions of all shifts]
Let $\varphi \neq 0$ be continuous and periodic with $T_s\varphi = \lambda(s)\varphi$ for every $s$. Then $\varphi = \varphi(0)\, e_k$ for some integer $k$.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. $\varphi(0) \ne 0$.** Otherwise putting $x = s$ in $\varphi(x - s) = \lambda(s)\varphi(x)$ gives $0 = \lambda(s)\varphi(s)$; at a point $s_0$ where $\varphi(s_0)\ne0$ this forces $\lambda(s_0) = 0$, and then $\varphi(x - s_0) = 0$ for all $x$, so $\varphi \equiv 0$.

**Step 2. $\lambda$ is multiplicative.** $T_{s+t} = T_sT_t$ gives $\lambda(s+t)\varphi = \lambda(s)\lambda(t)\varphi$, so $\lambda(s + t) = \lambda(s)\lambda(t)$; also $\lambda(s) = \varphi(0)/\varphi(s)$ (put $x = s$), so $\lambda$ is continuous with $\lambda(0) = 1$.

**Step 3. $\lambda$ is differentiable.** Pick $\delta > 0$ small enough that $a = \int_0^\delta\lambda(t)\,dt \ne 0$ (possible since $\lambda \approx 1$ near $0$). Then $a\lambda(s) = \int_0^\delta\lambda(s + t)\,dt = \int_s^{s+\delta}\lambda(u)\,du$, and the right side is differentiable in $s$.

**Step 4. Solve.** Differentiating Step 3, $a\lambda'(s) = \lambda(s + \delta) - \lambda(s) = \lambda(s)\big(\lambda(\delta) - 1\big)$. So $\lambda' = c\lambda$ with a constant $c$, and $\lambda(s) = e^{cs}$.

**Step 5. Periodicity.** $T_1\varphi = \varphi$ forces $\lambda(1) = 1$, so $e^{c} = 1$ and $c = 2\pi i m$ for an integer $m$. Then $\varphi(s) = \varphi(0)/\lambda(s) = \varphi(0)e^{-2\pi i m s} = \varphi(0)\,e_{-m}(s)$.
:::

:::remark[The general principle]
Shifts form a group (the circle here, the line in Section 6, the sphere in [the sphere post](/aboutme/blog/fourier-and-wavelets-on-the-sphere/)). The functions that every element of the group merely rescales are the group's **characters**, and expanding in them is harmonic analysis on that group. On the circle the characters are $e_k$; on the line, $e^{2\pi i\xi x}$; on the sphere, rotations no longer commute and the role is taken by the spherical harmonics. Wherever there is symmetry, there is a Fourier transform adapted to it.
:::

## 6. From series to transform

A signal on the whole line is not periodic. The trick is to pretend it is, with a period $L$ so large that the copies never meet, and to see what happens to the series as $L \to \infty$.

**The heuristic, step by step.** Let $f$ vanish outside $[-\tfrac L2, \tfrac L2]$ and repeat it with period $L$. Rescaling the formulas of Section 3 to period $L$ (substitute $x \to x/L$), the basis is $e^{2\pi i (k/L) x}$ and

$$
c_k = \frac1L\int_{-L/2}^{L/2} f(x)\,e^{-2\pi i (k/L) x}\,dx = \frac1L\,F\!\left(\frac kL\right), \quad \text{where } F(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-2\pi i \xi x}\,dx .
$$

The series becomes $f(x) = \sum_k F(k/L)\,e^{2\pi i (k/L)x}\cdot\frac1L$: a Riemann sum with spacing $\Delta\xi = 1/L$. As $L \to \infty$ it tends to $\int F(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i\xi x}\,d\xi$. The frequencies stop being whole numbers and fill the line.

:::definition[Fourier transform]
For $f$ integrable on $\mathbb{R}$,
$$
\hat f(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i \xi x}\, dx, \qquad f(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat f(\xi)\, e^{2\pi i \xi x}\, d\xi .
$$
The second formula, the **inversion theorem**, holds whenever $f$ is continuous and $\hat f$ is integrable; Section 7 proves it.
:::

$\hat f(\xi)$ is the same coordinate as before: the inner product of $f$ with the wave $e^{2\pi i\xi x}$, now for every real frequency $\xi$.

:::example[Three transforms by hand]
**A box.** $\operatorname{rect}(x) = 1$ for $\lvert x\rvert < \tfrac12$:
$\hat f(\xi) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx = \frac{e^{-\pi i\xi} - e^{\pi i\xi}}{-2\pi i\xi} = \frac{-2i\sin\pi\xi}{-2\pi i\xi} = \frac{\sin\pi\xi}{\pi\xi} = \operatorname{sinc}\xi$, with value $1$ at $\xi = 0$ (the area of the box).

**A two-sided exponential.** $f(x) = e^{-\lvert x\rvert}$. Split at zero: $\int_0^\infty e^{-x(1 + 2\pi i\xi)}dx = \frac{1}{1 + 2\pi i\xi}$ and $\int_{-\infty}^0 e^{x(1 - 2\pi i\xi)}dx = \frac{1}{1 - 2\pi i\xi}$. Add over the common denominator $1 + 4\pi^2\xi^2$: $\hat f(\xi) = \frac{2}{1 + 4\pi^2\xi^2}$, a Lorentzian. (A kink at $0$, so decay like $1/\xi^2$, as in Section 4.)

**A spike.** The box of width $\varepsilon$ and height $1/\varepsilon$ has area $1$ and, by the scaling rule of Section 7, transform $\operatorname{sinc}(\varepsilon\xi)$. As $\varepsilon \to 0$ the box becomes the Dirac delta and its transform becomes the constant $1$. **A perfect spike contains every frequency equally.** (Distribution theory makes this precise; Osgood's notes do it well.)

Each of these was checked numerically by quadrature at $\xi = 0.3$ and $1.7$.
:::

| Convention | Forward | Inverse |
|---|---|---|
| $\xi$, this series | $\int f e^{-2\pi i\xi x}$ | $\int \hat f e^{2\pi i\xi x}$ |
| $\omega$, physics | $\int f e^{-i\omega x}$ | $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int \hat f e^{i\omega x}$ |
| $\omega$, unitary | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int f e^{-i\omega x}$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\hat f e^{i\omega x}$ |

:::question
Which convention is right?
:::

:::answer
All three are; they differ by where $2\pi$ goes. With $\omega = 2\pi\xi$, the physics transform at $\omega$ equals ours at $\xi = \omega/2\pi$, and the unitary one is that divided by $\sqrt{2\pi}$. The cost of mixing them is a missing factor of $2\pi$ somewhere, usually in a Gaussian's width or in Plancherel's identity. Pick one and convert at the boundary of your code.
:::

## 7. Properties, each with a proof

Most Fourier calculations are a few of these rules applied in sequence. Assume $f$ and $g$ are integrable, and smooth and decaying wherever a derivative appears.

:::proposition[Shift, modulation, scaling]
1. **Shift:** $f(x - a) \;\longleftrightarrow\; e^{-2\pi i a\xi}\,\hat f(\xi)$.
2. **Modulation:** $e^{2\pi i b x}f(x) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \hat f(\xi - b)$.
3. **Scaling:** $f(ax) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\lvert a\rvert}\hat f\!\left(\frac{\xi}{a}\right)$ for $a \ne 0$.
:::

:::proof
**Shift.** Substitute $u = x - a$: $\int f(x - a)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx = \int f(u)e^{-2\pi i\xi(u + a)}du = e^{-2\pi i a\xi}\hat f(\xi)$.

**Modulation.** $\int e^{2\pi i bx}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx = \int f(x)e^{-2\pi i(\xi - b)x}dx = \hat f(\xi - b)$.

**Scaling, $a > 0$.** Substitute $u = ax$, $dx = du/a$: $\int f(ax)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx = \frac1a\int f(u)e^{-2\pi i(\xi/a)u}du = \frac1a\hat f(\xi/a)$.

**Scaling, $a < 0$.** The same substitution reverses the limits of integration, which contributes a minus sign: $-\frac1a\hat f(\xi/a) = \frac{1}{\lvert a\rvert}\hat f(\xi/a)$.
:::

The shift rule says that **moving a signal changes only the phase of its transform**, never the magnitude. That is why the magnitude spectrum of an image does not care where things are, and why positions are written in the phase (Section 10). The scaling rule says that **squeezing a signal stretches its spectrum**:

:::proposition[Derivatives and multiplication by x]
1. $f'(x) \;\longleftrightarrow\; 2\pi i\xi\,\hat f(\xi)$.
2. $x f(x) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{i}{2\pi}\,\hat f\,'(\xi)$.
:::

:::proof
**Derivative.** Integrate by parts: $\int f'(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx = \big[f(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + 2\pi i\xi\int f(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx$. The boundary term vanishes because $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$.

**Multiplication by $x$.** Differentiate under the integral sign (allowed when $xf(x)$ is integrable): $\hat f\,'(\xi) = \int f(x)\,(-2\pi i x)\,e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx = -2\pi i\,\widehat{xf}(\xi)$. Divide by $-2\pi i$, and $\frac{1}{-2\pi i} = \frac{i}{2\pi}$.
:::

These two rules turn calculus into algebra, and they give the most important pair of all in four lines.

:::proposition[The Gaussian is its own transform]
$e^{-\pi x^2} \;\longleftrightarrow\; e^{-\pi\xi^2}$.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1. The Gaussian solves an ODE.** $g(x) = e^{-\pi x^2}$ satisfies $g'(x) = -2\pi x\,g(x)$.

**Step 2. Transform both sides.** The left becomes $2\pi i\xi\,\hat g(\xi)$. The right becomes $-2\pi\cdot\frac{i}{2\pi}\hat g\,'(\xi) = -i\,\hat g\,'(\xi)$. So $\hat g\,'(\xi) = -2\pi\xi\,\hat g(\xi)$: the same ODE.

**Step 3. Solve it.** $\frac{d}{d\xi}\big(\hat g(\xi)e^{\pi\xi^2}\big) = \big(\hat g\,' + 2\pi\xi\hat g\big)e^{\pi\xi^2} = 0$, so $\hat g(\xi) = \hat g(0)\,e^{-\pi\xi^2}$.

**Step 4. The constant.** $\hat g(0) = \int e^{-\pi x^2}dx = I$. Square it and use polar coordinates: $I^2 = \iint e^{-\pi(x^2 + y^2)}dx\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-\pi r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi} = 1$. Since $I > 0$, $I = 1$.
:::

With scaling: $e^{-\pi x^2/s^2} \leftrightarrow s\,e^{-\pi s^2\xi^2}$, the pair in the clip above. Now the inversion theorem, which so far we only made plausible.

:::theorem[Fourier inversion]
If $f$ is integrable and continuous and $\hat f$ is integrable, then $f(x) = \int\hat f(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i\xi x}\,d\xi$ for every $x$.
:::

:::proof
The idea: insert a Gaussian damping factor to make every integral absolutely convergent, compute, and let the damping go.

**Step 1. Damp.** For $\varepsilon > 0$ let $I_\varepsilon(x) = \int\hat f(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i\xi x}\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}d\xi$. Since $\lvert\hat f\rvert$ is integrable and the damping is at most $1$ and tends to $1$, dominated convergence gives $I_\varepsilon(x) \to \int\hat f(\xi)e^{2\pi i\xi x}d\xi$.

**Step 2. Expand $\hat f$ and swap integrals.** $I_\varepsilon(x) = \int f(y)\Big(\int e^{-2\pi i\xi(y - x)}e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}d\xi\Big)dy$. The swap is allowed (Fubini) because $\iint\lvert f(y)\rvert e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}\,dy\,d\xi = \lVert f\rVert_1/\varepsilon < \infty$.

**Step 3. The inner integral is a Gaussian's transform.** It is the transform of $\xi \mapsto e^{-\pi(\varepsilon\xi)^2}$ evaluated at $y - x$. By the Gaussian pair and the scaling rule with $a = \varepsilon$, it equals $G_\varepsilon(y - x)$ with $G_\varepsilon(u) = \frac1\varepsilon e^{-\pi u^2/\varepsilon^2}$.

**Step 4. So $I_\varepsilon = f * G_\varepsilon$** ($G_\varepsilon$ is even).

**Step 5. $G_\varepsilon$ is an approximate identity.** It is positive; it integrates to $1$ (substitute $u = \varepsilon v$ and use Step 4 of the Gaussian proof); and for any $\delta > 0$, $\sup_{\lvert u\rvert>\delta}G_\varepsilon(u) = \frac1\varepsilon e^{-\pi\delta^2/\varepsilon^2} \to 0$, as does $\int_{\lvert u\rvert > \delta}G_\varepsilon$.

**Step 6. Convergence.** $\lvert(f*G_\varepsilon)(x) - f(x)\rvert \le \int G_\varepsilon(u)\lvert f(x - u) - f(x)\rvert du$. Choose $\delta$ so that $\lvert f(x-u) - f(x)\rvert < \eta$ for $\lvert u\rvert < \delta$ (continuity at $x$). The near part is below $\eta$. The far part is at most $\sup_{\lvert u\rvert > \delta}G_\varepsilon\cdot\lVert f\rVert_1 + \lvert f(x)\rvert\int_{\lvert u\rvert>\delta}G_\varepsilon$, which tends to $0$ by Step 5.

**Step 7. Compare the limits.** Step 1 and Step 6 compute the limit of the same $I_\varepsilon(x)$, so they agree.
:::

:::proposition[Plancherel and the convolution theorem]
1. **Plancherel:** $\int\lvert f(x)\rvert^2dx = \int\lvert\hat f(\xi)\rvert^2d\xi$. Energy is the same in both domains.
2. **Convolution:** $\widehat{f * g}(\xi) = \hat f(\xi)\,\hat g(\xi)$.
3. **Duality:** $\hat{\hat f}(x) = f(-x)$.
:::

:::proof
**Plancherel.** *Step 1, the multiplication formula.* For integrable $f, g$: $\int f(x)\hat g(x)\,dx = \iint f(x)g(y)e^{-2\pi i xy}dy\,dx = \int\hat f(y)g(y)\,dy$, by Fubini, reading the double integral both ways. *Step 2, choose $g = \overline{\hat f}$.* Then $\hat g(x) = \int\overline{\hat f(y)}e^{-2\pi i xy}dy = \overline{\int\hat f(y)e^{2\pi ixy}dy} = \overline{f(x)}$ by inversion. *Step 3, substitute:* $\int f\overline{f} = \int\hat f\,\overline{\hat f}$. (By density this extends to all of $L^2$, the content of Plancherel's theorem.)

**Convolution.** $\widehat{f*g}(\xi) = \int\!\!\int f(y)g(x - y)\,dy\,e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx$. Write $e^{-2\pi i\xi x} = e^{-2\pi i\xi y}e^{-2\pi i\xi(x-y)}$, swap integrals (Fubini), and substitute $u = x - y$ in the inner one: $\int f(y)e^{-2\pi i\xi y}\Big(\int g(u)e^{-2\pi i\xi u}du\Big)dy = \hat f(\xi)\hat g(\xi)$.

**Duality.** Inversion at $-x$: $f(-x) = \int\hat f(\xi)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}d\xi$, which is the transform of $\hat f$ evaluated at $x$.
:::

| Operation on $f$ | Effect on $\hat f$ |
|---|---|
| shift by $a$ | multiply by $e^{-2\pi i a\xi}$ (phase only) |
| multiply by $e^{2\pi i bx}$ | shift by $b$ |
| squeeze by $a$ | stretch by $a$, scale by $1/\lvert a\rvert$ |
| differentiate | multiply by $2\pi i\xi$ |
| multiply by $x$ | $\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{d}{d\xi}$ |
| convolve with $g$ | multiply by $\hat g$ |
| real-valued | $\hat f(-\xi) = \overline{\hat f(\xi)}$ |

The last row is the negative-frequency proposition of Section 3 on the line, with the same proof.

## 8. Filters and what "frequency response" means

A **linear shift-invariant system** (in engineering, an LTI system) takes a signal $x$ and returns $y = Lx$, linearly and treating every time alike. Section 5 already told us everything about it: it is a convolution, $y = h * x$, with $h = L\delta$ the **impulse response**, and in frequency it multiplies, $\hat y(\xi) = \hat h(\xi)\,\hat x(\xi)$. The function $\hat h$ is the **frequency response**: by how much, and with what phase shift, the system passes each frequency.

To see filters act, the series uses one shared signal, **the song**: 2048 samples over one second, containing a chord (two close tones, 40 and 56 Hz), then a chirp sweeping from 60 to 240 Hz, then a single click. It is synthetic, so every number about it is exact.

:::question
"A filter removes frequencies." Why is that not quite true, and what does the ringing in the figure have to do with Section 4?
:::

:::answer
An ideal low-pass filter has $\hat h = \operatorname{rect}(\xi/2B)$: one inside $[-B, B]$, zero outside. By the box pair and duality its impulse response is $h(x) = 2B\operatorname{sinc}(2Bx)$, which has negative side lobes. Convolving a click with it reproduces those lobes: ripples on both sides. This is the Gibbs phenomenon again, now in time: chopping a spectrum sharply is convolving with a kernel that rings. A Gaussian response has a Gaussian, positive impulse response, so it smooths without ringing; the price is a gentle roll-off instead of a clean cut. Every real filter trades sharpness in frequency against compactness and positivity in time, a first glimpse of the uncertainty principle of Part 2.
:::

Two more facts worth keeping. Since a shift is multiplication by $e^{-2\pi i a\xi}$, a filter whose phase is linear in $\xi$ just **delays** the signal; any other phase distorts it, which is why image filters are usually designed to be symmetric (zero phase). And a derivative is the filter $2\pi i\xi$: a high-pass that grows without bound, which is why differentiating noisy data amplifies the noise.

## 9. Images: the 2-D Fourier transform

Everything carries over to two dimensions, with one new ingredient: **direction**.

:::definition[2-D Fourier transform]
For an image $f(\boldsymbol{x})$, $\boldsymbol{x} = (x, y)$,
$$
\hat f(\boldsymbol{\xi}) = \iint f(\boldsymbol{x})\, e^{-2\pi i\,\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}\, d\boldsymbol{x}, \qquad f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \iint\hat f(\boldsymbol{\xi})\,e^{2\pi i\,\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi},
$$
with $\boldsymbol{\xi} = (\xi, \eta)$ and $\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi} = x\xi + y\eta$.
:::

The basis functions are **plane waves**, $e^{2\pi i\,\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}$. Their phase is constant wherever $\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is, which is along lines **perpendicular** to $\boldsymbol{\xi}$; moving a distance $d$ along $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ changes $\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}$ by $d\,\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rvert$, so the wavelength is $1/\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rvert$. A single point $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ of the spectrum, together with its mirror $-\boldsymbol{\xi}$ (the image is real), is one **grating**: stripes perpendicular to $\boldsymbol{\xi}$, spaced $1/\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rvert$ apart, with an amplitude and a phase.

:::proposition[Separability and rotation]
1. **Separability:** if $f(x, y) = g(x)\,h(y)$, then $\hat f(\xi, \eta) = \hat g(\xi)\,\hat h(\eta)$.
2. **Rotation:** for a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}$, $f(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{x}) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \hat f(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\xi})$. Rotate an image and its spectrum rotates with it.
:::

:::proof
**Separability.** $e^{-2\pi i(x\xi + y\eta)} = e^{-2\pi i x\xi}e^{-2\pi i y\eta}$, so the double integral splits into $\int g(x)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}dx\cdot\int h(y)e^{-2\pi iy\eta}dy$ (Fubini).

**Rotation.** Substitute $\boldsymbol{u} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{x}$. A rotation has $\lvert\det\mathbf{R}\rvert = 1$, so $d\boldsymbol{x} = d\boldsymbol{u}$, and $\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, so $\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi} = (\mathbf{R}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi} = \boldsymbol{u}\cdot(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\xi})$. Hence $\iint f(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{x})e^{-2\pi i\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}d\boldsymbol{x} = \iint f(\boldsymbol{u})e^{-2\pi i\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\xi}}d\boldsymbol{u} = \hat f(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\xi})$.
:::

A consequence you can see in every image spectrum: **a pattern that varies in one direction puts its energy on a line through the origin in that direction**, perpendicular to its stripes. If $f(x, y) = g(x)$ does not depend on $y$, separability with $h = 1$ (whose transform is a spike at $\eta = 0$) puts all of $\hat f$ on the horizontal axis. In the explorer below, choose *Sunset*: the horizon and the waves are horizontal, they vary vertically, and the spectrum has a bright **vertical** streak. Its strongest off-centre wave, found by the figure, is four horizontal stripes across the image.

### The projection–slice theorem

One 2-D result deserves a place here because a machine in every hospital depends on it.

:::theorem[Projection–slice]
Let $p(x) = \int f(x, y)\,dy$ be the projection of an image onto the $x$-axis. Then $\hat p(\xi) = \hat f(\xi, 0)$: the 1-D transform of a projection is a **slice** of the 2-D transform through the origin. By the rotation theorem, the same holds at every angle.
:::

:::proof
**Step 1.** $\hat p(\xi) = \int\Big(\int f(x, y)\,dy\Big)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}dx$.

**Step 2.** Insert $e^{-2\pi iy\cdot 0} = 1$: $\hat p(\xi) = \iint f(x, y)\,e^{-2\pi i(x\xi + y\cdot 0)}\,dx\,dy = \hat f(\xi, 0)$.

**Step 3. Other angles.** Projecting along direction $\theta$ is projecting the rotated image $f(\mathbf{R}_\theta\boldsymbol{x})$ onto the $x$-axis; by the rotation theorem its 2-D transform is $\hat f(\mathbf{R}_\theta\boldsymbol{\xi})$, whose slice at $\eta = 0$ is $\hat f$ along the rotated axis.
:::

A CT scanner measures X-ray projections of a body at many angles. Each projection, transformed, fills one line of the 2-D spectrum; enough angles fill it all, and an inverse 2-D transform (in practice, its rearrangement as *filtered back-projection*) gives the image. MRI goes further: it measures the spectrum $\hat f$ directly, one line of "k-space" at a time.

The image with the *Portrait*'s amplitudes and the *Tree*'s phases looks like the tree: its correlation with the tree is $0.81$, with the portrait $0.16$. Keep the portrait's amplitudes and draw the phases at random, and the correlation with the portrait falls to $0.05$: a texture with the right mixture of scales and nothing in place. Oppenheim and Lim made this observation famous in 1981, and it is easy to underrate.

Why phase? By the shift rule, position lives in the phase. And edges are places where many frequencies agree. Look at the square wave's series, $\frac4\pi\sum\frac{\sin 2\pi kx}{k}$: at the jump $x = 0$, *every* term crosses zero going upward at the same moment. An edge is a **phase alignment** across frequencies (Morrone and Owens call it phase congruency), and the amplitudes, which are much alike from one natural image to the next, only say how strong each scale is.

:::note[What this means in practice]
Discarding phase is a common way to lose information without noticing: a magnitude spectrogram, a power spectrum used as a feature, a loss computed on $\lvert\hat f\rvert$ only. Recovering a signal from magnitudes alone, **phase retrieval**, is a field of its own, central to X-ray crystallography and coherent diffraction imaging.
:::

## 11. What Fourier cannot tell you

Go back to the song. Its spectrum has two sharp peaks for the chord, a broad plateau for the chirp, and a faint floor that covers every frequency. That floor is the click: a spike contains every frequency equally (Section 6). The spectrum is a perfect inventory, and it is silent about **when**. Nothing in $\lvert\hat x(\xi)\rvert$ says that the chirp rises rather than falls, or that the click comes last. The same is true of the edge of an eyelid in a sketch: it is everywhere in the spectrum and nowhere in particular.

This is not a defect of the computation. Every $e^{2\pi i\xi x}$ stretches over the whole line, so every coefficient is a global average. To say *where* and *when*, we need building blocks that are local in both position and frequency. ## 12. Implementation and caveats

In practice the integrals of this post are computed with the FFT, an algorithm for the discrete Fourier transform $X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-2\pi ikn/N}$ (Part 2 derives it). Two recipes cover most needs.

**Fourier series coefficients.** Sample one period at $x_n = n/N$. The rectangle rule turns $c_k = \int_0^1 f(x)e^{-2\pi ikx}dx$ into $\frac1N\sum_n f(x_n)e^{-2\pi ikn/N} = X[k]/N$.

```python
import numpy as np

N = 4096
x = np.arange(N) / N                     # one period, [0, 1)
f = np.where(x < 0.5, 1.0, -1.0)         # the square wave of Section 3
c = np.fft.fft(f) / N                    # c[k] ~ c_k for 0 <= k < N/2, and c[N - k] ~ c_{-k}
print(c[1], 2 / (np.pi * 1j))            # both about -0.6366j
```

**The Fourier transform on a grid.** Sample $f$ at $x_n = x_0 + n\Delta x$. At the frequencies $\xi_k = k/(N\Delta x)$, a Riemann sum gives $\hat f(\xi_k) \approx \Delta x\sum_n f(x_n)e^{-2\pi i\xi_k(x_0 + n\Delta x)} = \Delta x\,e^{-2\pi i\xi_k x_0}\,X[k]$, because $\xi_k n\Delta x = kn/N$.

```python
n, L = 512, 16.0
dx = L / n
xg = -L / 2 + dx * np.arange(n)                             # the grid starts at x0 = -8, not at 0
f = np.exp(-np.pi * (xg - 1.0) ** 2)                        # a Gaussian centred at x = 1
xi = np.fft.fftfreq(n, d=dx)                                # frequencies in cycles per unit
F = dx * np.exp(-2j * np.pi * xi * xg[0]) * np.fft.fft(f)   # note the phase factor for x0
exact = np.exp(-np.pi * xi**2) * np.exp(-2j * np.pi * xi)   # Gaussian pair + shift rule
print(np.abs(F - exact).max())                              # about 1e-15
```

Both snippets were run as written. The traps, each with its symptom:

1. **The origin phase.** Drop the factor $e^{-2\pi i\xi_kx_0}$ and the magnitude is still exactly right but the phase is wrong by up to $\pi$ (measured: magnitude error $2\times10^{-16}$, phase error $3.14$ rad). A plot of $\lvert\hat f\rvert$ will never reveal this bug.
2. **Conventions.** NumPy uses the DFT above, with $1/N$ on the inverse; `norm="ortho"` puts $1/\sqrt N$ on both. Mixing $\xi$ and $\omega$ costs a factor of $2\pi$ (Section 6).
3. **Frequency order.** The FFT returns $k = 0, 1, \ldots, N/2 - 1$, then the negative frequencies. Use `fftfreq` for the values and `fftshift` to centre zero for display.
4. **Real input.** A real signal has $\hat f(-\xi) = \overline{\hat f(\xi)}$: half the output is redundant. `rfft` returns only the non-negative half and is about twice as fast.
5. **Truncation rings.** Cutting a spectrum sharply, to compress or to denoise, rings near edges (Gibbs). Taper the cut.
6. **Differentiation by FFT** multiplies by $2\pi i\xi$: superb for smooth periodic data, disastrous for noisy data or data with jumps, where it amplifies noise and Gibbs ripples.
7. **Display.** Show $\log(1 + \lvert\hat f\rvert)$; raw magnitudes are a single bright dot. Phase is meaningless where the magnitude is near zero, so mask it there.

Two issues are deliberately left for Part 2: why the FFT of a function that is not periodic on its window shows a bright cross ("leakage"), and why sampling too coarsely silently changes frequencies ("aliasing").

## 13. Where it is used, and what is open

| Use Fourier analysis when | Be careful when |
|---|---|
| the operation is linear and shift-invariant (blur, convolution, differentiation, constant-coefficient PDEs) | the signal changes character over time or space (speech, a chirp, an image with distinct regions) |
| the domain is periodic, or can be made so | there are boundaries the method does not know about |
| you need global structure: dominant orientations, textures, periodicity | you need to locate a localised event |
| speed matters: convolution with large kernels, solving Poisson's equation | the data has jumps and you need pointwise accuracy |

**Where it shows up in current work.** Spectral methods for PDEs on periodic domains; CT and MRI reconstruction, including from undersampled k-space; phase correlation for image registration; Fourier features and positional encodings in neural fields (Tancik et al., 2020), together with the observation that networks learn low frequencies first, *spectral bias* (Rahaman et al., 2019); global filtering layers in vision networks (GFNet); Fourier neural operators for learning PDE solution maps (Li et al., 2021); FFT-based long convolutions in sequence models.

**Open questions** worth a PhD's attention:
1. **Spectral bias, made predictive.** Networks fit low frequencies first. Can we predict, from an architecture and its initialisation, which frequencies it will learn and how fast, and choose encodings accordingly instead of by trial?
2. **Phase retrieval with learned priors.** Recovering an image from Fourier magnitudes is ill-posed in general; learned priors make it work in practice. When is the answer unique and stable, and can the prior's errors be detected?
3. **Fourier methods off the grid.** FFT speed needs regular grids and periodic boundaries. What is the right fast spectral layer on irregular meshes, point clouds or bounded domains, without losing the diagonalisation of Section 5?
4. **When does global spectral mixing beat attention or convolution** in vision models, and why? Current evidence is empirical and task-specific.
5. **Trusting individual frequencies.** A learned surrogate may be accurate at low frequencies and wrong at high ones. How should uncertainty be reported per frequency band, so that a user knows which scales of a prediction to believe?

## Self-check

:::question
You shift an image by 10 pixels. What happens to $\lvert\hat f\rvert$, and what happens to the phase?
:::

:::answer
By the shift rule, $\hat f$ is multiplied by $e^{-2\pi i\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}$ with $\boldsymbol{a}$ the shift. The magnitude is unchanged; the phase gains a linear ramp $-2\pi\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}$. (On a finite image the shift must be circular for this to be exact; Part 2.)
:::

:::question
Show that a real, even function has a real, even Fourier transform.
:::

:::answer
**Step 1.** $\hat f(\xi) = \int f(x)\cos(2\pi\xi x)dx - i\int f(x)\sin(2\pi\xi x)dx$. **Step 2.** $f(x)\sin(2\pi\xi x)$ is odd in $x$ when $f$ is even, so its integral over the line is $0$: $\hat f$ is real. **Step 3.** $\cos(2\pi\xi x)$ is even in $\xi$, so $\hat f(-\xi) = \hat f(\xi)$.
:::

:::question
Why does the square wave have no even harmonics?
:::

:::answer
It satisfies $f(x + \tfrac12) = -f(x)$. Then $c_k = \int_0^1 f(x)e^{-2\pi ikx}dx$, and substituting $x \to x + \tfrac12$ gives $c_k = \int_0^1 f(x + \tfrac12)e^{-2\pi ik(x + 1/2)}dx = -(-1)^k c_k$. For even $k$ this says $c_k = -c_k$, so $c_k = 0$.
:::

:::question
Which decays faster: the Fourier coefficients of the 1-periodic extension of $x$ on $[-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, or of $\lvert x\rvert$?
:::

:::answer
$\lvert x\rvert$: its periodic extension is continuous, with kinks, so its coefficients fall like $1/k^2$. The extension of $x$ jumps at $\pm\tfrac12$, so its coefficients fall only like $1/k$ (Section 4).
:::

:::question
You blur an image with a Gaussian of standard deviation $\sigma$ pixels. By what factor is the amplitude of a grating of frequency $\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rvert$ (cycles per pixel) reduced?
:::

:::answer
The kernel $\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\rvert^2/2\sigma^2}$ is separable; in each direction, by the Gaussian pair and scaling ($e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2} = e^{-\pi(x/s)^2}$ with $s = \sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$), its normalised transform is $e^{-\pi s^2\xi^2} = e^{-2\pi^2\sigma^2\xi^2}$. In 2-D the factor is $e^{-2\pi^2\sigma^2\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rvert^2}$. Energy, the square, falls by $e^{-4\pi^2\sigma^2\lvert\boldsymbol{\xi}\rvert^2}$.
:::

:::question
A real cosine of amplitude $1$ is sampled at $N$ points and passed to an FFT. Why are there two peaks of height $N/2$ instead of one of height $N$?
:::

:::answer
By Section 3, $\cos\theta = \tfrac12e^{i\theta} + \tfrac12e^{-i\theta}$: half the amplitude sits at $+k_0$ and half at $-k_0$, and the DFT stores $-k_0$ at index $N - k_0$. Each phasor contributes $N\cdot\tfrac12$ through a geometric sum; Part 2 carries out the sum.
:::
